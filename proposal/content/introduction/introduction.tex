Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) grounds a large language model (LLM) in documents retrieved from an external corpus \citep{lewis2020rag,gao2023ragsurvey}. Its answers are bounded by its retriever: if the document that answers the question is not retrieved, the generator cannot use it and may be misled by irrelevant context \citep{shi2023distractors}.

Two retrieval signals are available, with opposite strengths. \emph{Dense retrievers} rank documents by the cosine similarity of their embeddings to the query embedding \citep{karpukhin2020dpr,reimers2019sbert}. This is almost free with a vector index \citep{malkov2020hnsw}, but on our biomedical benchmark the top document is correct for only 70\% of the questions. \emph{LLM judges} rate relevance in close agreement with human assessors \citep{thomas2024llmjudge}. Yet every judgement is a costly LLM call, and labels on a small integer scale give many documents the same top score \citep{zhuang2024beyond}. Existing methods use the expensive signal passively. Re-rankers re-order a fixed top-$k$ list \citep{nogueira2019passage,sun2023rankgpt}: they pay $k$ calls even when the retriever is right and cannot reach a target outside the list. Query rewriting and pseudo-relevance feedback move the retrieval anchor without true feedback \citep{rocchio1971relevance,gao2023hyde}. Adaptive RAG detects \emph{that} retrieval failed, not \emph{where} to look next \citep{yan2024crag}. Moreover, embedding spaces are anisotropic \citep{ethayarajh2019contextual}, and two-tower models can place their two input types in separate regions \citep{liang2022modalitygap}, so a question may lie far from the documents that answer it.

This research, developed in our supervision meetings, treats \emph{judge scores as feedback that steers a search through the embedding space}. Each judged document indicates where the target lies, as in the word game Semantle or in trilateration from known anchors. Retrieval with a judge thus becomes the classical problem of optimising an expensive evaluation function with a cheap surrogate under a budget, as in best-first search \citep{pearl1984heuristics} and Bayesian optimisation \citep{shahriari2016bayesopt}. To our knowledge, no prior work studies the LLM judge as an expensive oracle inside a sequential search over the embedding space, with the number of judge calls as the cost and the query--document geometry as the explanation. This is the gap we address.
