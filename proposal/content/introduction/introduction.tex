Large language models (LLMs) store knowledge in their parameters. That knowledge is fixed at training time and incomplete in specialised domains. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) addresses this by retrieving documents from an external corpus at inference time and conditioning the LLM on them \citep{lewis2020rag,gao2023ragsurvey}. A RAG pipeline is only as good as its retriever: if the document that answers the question is not retrieved, the generator either cannot answer or answers from irrelevant context, which can actively mislead it \citep{shi2023distractors}. In biomedicine, where RAG is increasingly used for clinical and scientific question answering \citep{xiong2024medrag}, such failures can lead to confidently wrong answers.

Two kinds of retrieval signal are available today, and they behave very differently.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Embedding similarity.} Dense bi-encoders embed queries and documents in a shared vector space and rank documents by cosine similarity to the query \citep{karpukhin2020dpr,reimers2019sbert}. With an approximate nearest-neighbour index the ranking is almost free \citep{malkov2020hnsw}. It is also systematically imperfect: on our challenging biomedical benchmark the top-ranked document is the correct one for only 70\% of the questions (Chapter~\ref{chapter:preliminary}).
  \item \textbf{LLM judgement.} An LLM prompted to rate the relevance of a document to a question separates relevant from irrelevant documents almost perfectly \citep{thomas2024llmjudge,faggioli2023perspectives}. However, each judgement is an LLM call, and its integer scale produces large groups of documents with the same top score \citep{zhuang2024beyond}.
\end{itemize}

The obvious combination, retrieving the top-$k$ documents and re-ranking them with the LLM, uses the judge as a passive scorer of a fixed list. That approach pays for $k$ calls whether or not the retriever was already right. It cannot reach a target that lies outside the initial list, and it inherits the judge's ties. This research starts from a different view, developed during our supervision meetings: \emph{the judge's scores are feedback that can steer a search through the embedding space}. Each judged document tells us something about where the target lies. This resembles the word game Semantle, where a player guesses a hidden word from the semantic similarity of previous guesses, and trilateration, where a position is recovered from distances to known anchors. The research question then becomes algorithmic: \emph{given a cheap but imperfect geometric surrogate and an accurate but expensive judge, which document should be judged next, so that the target is found with as few judge calls as possible?}

\paragraph{What we have learned so far.}
Over the past two years we built the experimental infrastructure for this question: a full-PubMed dense index, a cloud and cluster pipeline that scored about one million question--document pairs with an LLM judge, and an offline simulator that replays any search policy against these scores at no additional cost. With this infrastructure we established the following (Chapter~\ref{chapter:preliminary}):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The judge's score is a reliable but coarse signal of proximity to the target document. The correlation with distance is negative for every query and ROC-AUC is 0.997, yet a median of 18 documents per query share the top score.
  \item Queries are geometric outliers relative to documents. The neighbourhood of a query is almost empty, while the neighbourhood of its target document is populated.
  \item The centroid of the documents the judge approves is 44\% closer to the target than the query is. Nevertheless, \emph{static} heuristics that use it improve top-1 accuracy by at most one percentage point, and \emph{naive} online steering towards it increases the number of judge calls.
\end{enumerate}
Together, these results show that the geometric signal is real, that it is not exploited by simply mixing distances, and that the open problem is a well-posed search problem.

\paragraph{Research questions.}
The proposed research addresses five questions, detailed in Chapter~\ref{chapter:objectives}. How reliable is the LLM judge as a proximity signal, and can its resolution be improved (RQ1)? What geometric properties distinguish queries on which dense retrieval fails (RQ2)? Can judge-guided search policies find the target with fewer calls than the retriever's order (RQ3)? Can failure be detected early enough to invoke the search only when needed (RQ4)? Do the findings generalise across embedders, judges and datasets (RQ5)?
